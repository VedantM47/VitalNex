## 1. System overview

**What it is.** VITALNEX is a multilingual (English / Hindi / Marathi) screening and
care-navigation **MVP** for patients and clinicians. A person answers 14 self-reported questions
(plus optional symptom ticks and free text). For three conditions (diabetes/prediabetes, heart
disease or heart attack, stroke) VITALNEX shows how similar the answers are to people who have been
told they have the condition, as a level (Low / Moderate / High, or "not confident — see a
doctor"), the main factors, what the person could change, and what kind of care to seek and how
soon.

**What it is not.** Not a diagnosis, not a prescription, not a replacement for a doctor, not
validated on Indian patients. All accounts are dummy demo accounts.

**How a result is made** (SPEC §1.3, diagram 3 in `ARCHITECTURE_DIAGRAMS.md`):

1. Validation (Pydantic) → **emergency rule** (symptom ticks or red-flag words in free text; if
   triggered, no model runs and only the 108 / 112 card is shown) → **data-quality gate**.
2. Three small models, one per condition, trained on CDC BRFSS 2015 (253,680 rows,
   `stats.json`). Production models (`model_meta.json: chosen_model`): diabetes **LightGBM**,
   heart **LightGBM**, stroke **logistic regression**, each with isotonic calibration fitted on a
   separate calibration split (cal-A).
3. **Conformal abstention** (Mondrian split conformal, α = 0.10) plus an **IsolationForest
   out-of-distribution check**: if the model is not confident for a condition, no level is shown.
4. Deterministic **next-step rules** (SPEC §1.4, not the model): emergency → correct inputs →
   not confident → see a doctor soon (any High) → discuss at next check-up (any Moderate) →
   routine check-ups.
5. SHAP explanations (TreeExplainer for LightGBM, LinearExplainer for LR) mapped to fixed,
   pre-written sentences.
6. High and abstained results of patients who consented to share go to a **clinician review
   queue**; clinicians agree or override with a required reason; every override is logged.

**Assistant.** A local, frozen `l3cube-pune/indic-sentence-bert-nli` encoder (CC-BY-4.0) with a
small MLP intent head and a curated FAQ library (59 entries per language, `nlp_meta.json`). It only
returns fixed, pre-written replies. Emergency and medicine-request rules run before the classifier.

**Data flow.** All ML and NLP run on the VITALNEX server (CPU laptop). Supabase (Postgres + Auth,
Mumbai region) stores accounts, consents, assessments, the review queue and the audit log, with Row
Level Security. No health data is sent to any third-party API (hard rule 6).

## 4. Fairness findings

Definition (`model_meta.json: fairness.definition`): "flagged" = calibrated probability at or above
the Moderate threshold (Moderate or High band), test split. A recall gap above 0.10 is flagged
(SPEC §4.7). We report gaps; we do not claim compliance with any fairness rule.

### 4.1 Age: the largest gap (flagged for all three diseases)

| Disease | Recall 18–39 | Recall 40–59 | Recall 60+ | FPR 18–39 | FPR 60+ | Recall gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | 0.396 | 0.792 | 0.905 | 0.051 | 0.563 | 0.509 |
| Heart | 0.118 | 0.660 | 0.901 | 0.014 | 0.532 | 0.783 |
| Stroke | 0.170 | 0.634 | 0.862 | 0.014 | 0.548 | 0.692 |

Age is the strongest predictor for all three conditions and these conditions are rare in young
adults (observed rate in 18–39 on test: diabetes 3.2%, heart 1.2%, stroke 0.7%), so one shared
threshold misses most young cases and flags most older people.

**Mitigation tried: per-age-group Moderate thresholds** (`docs/fairness_mitigation.md`). Each age
group got its own threshold with recall ≥ 0.80 within that group on cal-B; results on test:

| Disease | 18–39 recall before → after | 18–39 FPR before → after | 18–39 flagged share before → after |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | 0.396 → 0.759 | 0.051 → 0.286 | 6.2% → 30.1% |
| Heart | 0.118 → 0.763 | 0.014 → 0.275 | 1.5% → 28.1% |
| Stroke | 0.170 → 0.792 | 0.014 → 0.397 | 1.6% → 40.0% |

**Why it was not adopted (D-24, approved 2026-10-28).** Young-group recall rose a lot, but the
false-positive rate in that group rose to 0.28–0.40, so 28–40% of young people would be flagged,
nearly all of them without the condition. Overall the share of people flagged also rose (heart
35.2% → 41.3%, stroke 34.1% → 44.0%). The team chose to disclose the gap instead: the Trust
Passport shows it, and every user under 40 sees "Results are less reliable for people under 40."

### 4.2 Income (flagged for all three diseases)

| Disease | Recall low (< $25k) | Recall high (≥ $50k) | FPR low | FPR high | Recall gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | 0.914 | 0.778 | 0.545 | 0.273 | 0.136 |
| Heart | 0.883 | 0.763 | 0.481 | 0.203 | 0.120 |
| Stroke | 0.887 | 0.643 | 0.554 | 0.193 | 0.244 |

The gap runs in an unusual direction: the models miss **more** cases among higher-income people and
give **more false alarms** to lower-income people. Income is not a model input (D-6); the gap
comes through correlated inputs (general health, BMI, high blood pressure, age). No mitigation was
tried; this is reported only.

### 4.3 Sex (flagged for heart only)

Heart recall: female 0.779, male 0.882 (gap 0.103, just above the 0.10 flag); FPR female 0.240,
male 0.381. Diabetes (gap 0.017) and stroke (gap 0.003) are not flagged. No mitigation was tried.
For "prefer not to say", VITALNEX runs both values and shows the higher level (D-9).

### 4.4 Other groups

Stroke shows an education recall gap of 0.110 (flagged). Healthcare access and "could not afford a
doctor" gaps are below 0.10 for all three diseases. Abstention also differs by age: for heart,
5.1% of 18–39 vs 57.2% of 60+ abstained (`fairness.groups.AgeGroup.by_group.*.abstention_rate`),
so older people more often get "not confident — see a doctor" instead of a level.

## 5. Limitations

1. **US survey data, Indian users.** Models are trained on BRFSS 2015 (US telephone survey,
   self-reported). Nothing has been validated on Indian people. Calibration will likely differ.
2. **Self-reported, "ever told" labels.** Targets are "ever told by a doctor" answers, not clinical
   diagnoses. Heart = coronary heart disease or heart attack. Undiagnosed people count as negatives.
3. **Duplicate rows.** 24,206 exact duplicate rows and 79,790 rows duplicated on the 14 features
   (`stats.json`). AUROC on a deduplicated test set is about 0.02 lower (§2.5).
4. **High abstention.** 36.8% (diabetes), 38.5% (heart) and 44.4% (stroke) of test people get no level for that condition (accepted,
   D-22). This is honest but limits usefulness.
5. **Low band still contains cases** (15–21% of true cases on test, D-23) and **young people are
   poorly served** (§4.1, D-24).
6. **Calibration did not always help.** For diabetes and heart, isotonic calibration did not
   improve the Brier score on cal-B (`flags` in `model_meta.json`: diabetes 0.0969 → 0.0971, heart
   0.0709 → 0.0710); it was kept for a uniform pipeline. For stroke (logistic regression) it did
   (0.1824 → 0.0366).
7. **Smoking is "ever smoked 100 cigarettes"** in BRFSS, so quitting cannot lower that input; the
   what-if panel says so.
8. **Assistant data is synthetic** (written by Claude Code), FAQ answers only about 58% of test
   queries (`faq.test_coverage.all.rate` = 0.5833) and the emergency rules were extended after
   seeing test misses (D-25).
9. **HI/MR medical wording is unreviewed** (D-15).
10. **No real users, no real clinicians.** All accounts and the demo history are synthetic; demo
    assessments are produced by running the real models on invented answers
    (`assessment_mode = 'demo'`).

## 7. Known gaps

- **Patients can read their own stored probabilities.** The API removes `probability`,
  `conformal_set` and `sex_used` from patient responses (D-10), but RLS lets a patient select their
  own `risk_assessments` rows, whose `results` JSON contains the probabilities. A patient using the
  Supabase client directly with their own JWT can read them. Fix: move probabilities to a
  clinician-only column or table. Not done.
- **Recourse usage is not recorded**, so the "Recourse usage" evidence is "not available".
- **HI/MR medical wording has not been reviewed** by a native-speaker clinician (D-15).
- **Nothing has been clicked through in a browser with Playwright.** Playwright is not installed;
  signed-in pages have not been smoke-tested end to end. Checks so far are API tests, lint and
  build (see `redteam_report.md` for the P10 results).
- **The team's own red-team run before the demo** (SPEC §7.4 gate 4) has not been signed off.
- **Demo history is thin**: one High case (patient1's latest visit) was added in P10 so the
  clinician-override part of the demo works; the jump from Low to High between visits reflects
  invented answers, not a real trajectory.

## 8. What would be needed before any real clinical use

1. **Validation on Indian data** (e.g. NFHS-5 via DHS Program registration, or a prospective
   local cohort with measured values) and recalibration on that population; re-run fairness by
   age, sex, region, language and socio-economic group.
2. **Regulatory review.** Any use beyond screening and navigation would need CDSCO review as
   medical-device software; the clinical-research use would need ethics approval under the ICMR
   guidelines for AI in biomedical research and healthcare (2023).
3. **DPDP Act 2023 / DPDP Rules 2025 compliance review** by a qualified person: consent notices,
   retention, breach process, grievance contact, data-processor agreement with the database host.
4. **Native-speaker clinical review** of all HI/MR wording and of the English guidance content.
5. **Clinician pilot** with real clinicians: workflow fit, automation-bias monitoring (agree
   rate, override reasons), and a process for acting on High results.
6. **Fix the known gaps** in §7 (probabilities readable by the patient; record recourse usage;
   end-to-end browser tests).
7. **Monitoring**: drift, abstention and override rates over time, with a human-owned process to
   retrain — never automatic retraining from overrides or user data (hard rule 9).
8. **A better solution for young adults** than a disclosure note (e.g. different inputs or a
   dedicated model), reviewed by the team.
9. **Security review** (penetration test of the API, RLS policies and share links) and real
   authentication policies (no shared demo password).
